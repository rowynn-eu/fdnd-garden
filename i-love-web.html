We ♥ Web: The Indie Web w/ Justus Sturkenboom

In this lecture, Justus explained his experience with the Internet growing up, how it differs from what it is today, and how he believes the right way is the combat the boring, corporate web.

He showcased some of his favourite websites on the IndieWeb and explained the concept of webrings, and how those indei websites are connected with one another. He mentioned Neocities, and even the older Geocities.

Really, the core of his message is to start owning your own content. This is best done by publishing on your own site, and syndicating elsewhere, meaning if you want to share your cool photos, blogposts, or releases, tweet about it with a link back to your own website.

That website could be a digital garden (see homepage...), a blog, or anything else really. It's your website!

I've chosen my favourite examples that he showcased and linked them here, they are definitely worth checking out.

  • nobodyhere.com
  • ribo.zone
  • solar.lowtechmagazine.com

We ♥ Web: werktijden.nl w/ Tom Poortman and Tom Deeterink

Their lecture was interesting. I was able to get insight into how their business runs, and how they deliver products to their clients. Prior to joining, one of these ex-students quit and became a product manager, where-as the other graduated and became a junior developer.

The company's workethic was... chaotic until they implemented a variant of scrum. They like attributing a certain amount of points to each issue to get an estimate on how long it'll take to resolve it, and then they start developing to resolve that problem.

The issue can be comprised of feedback from a client, or something they've noticed internally that they want to improve. Eitherway, they try to resolve it immediately, and determine how urgent it is. If it's a bug, it's number one priority.

Other cool insights were that they tend to listen to the feedback of the majority of clients, rather than the request of a single, larger client as they value networking and a steady clientbase more than making a single client happy.

They get a downside from me that accessibility is not a priority or interest in their company, and during their live-coding session we even spotted a bug about how their proposed solution of adding a hover state to see the weather on that day won't work for someone on mobile, as they don't have hover states there.

There was some takeaway though. A solid plan leads to a solid project board.

We ♥ Web: schiphol.nl w/ Ischa Gast

Ischa Gast, who is a front-end developer at schiphol.nl spoke today about accessibility. He showed us several videos on how accessibility can be meaningful, and fruitful for someone, and how you can improve as a developer. His key message is to is to fulfill Tim Burner Lee's message and to make the web accessibile.

To tab or not to tab: Key takeaways:

  1. The first tab on a page should be a skip link.
  2. HTML is accessible by default.
  3. First Rule of ARIA: Don't Use Aria.
  4. Describe what something does, and determine the right function.
  5. Is an element decorative or informative?
  6. Check the palette, not every screen.
  7. What you see, you have to hear.
  8. Text in a different language
  9. If something can be opened, you should also be able to close it without using a mouse.

Another thing he spoke about are the most common problems he'd find in an audit.

  1. If you see different kind of content, then they are tabs.
  2. If you have to pick one option, then they are radio buttons.
  3. If you have to turn something on or off, then it's a toggle.

I think it went by a bit too fast in my tastes that I had trouble following it all, but the findings of the talk that I can remember (and noted down) are the following...

  • 100% of people are disabled at some point.
  • Test websites with other people that have a disability.
  • You are never fully done when it comes to making a website accessible.
